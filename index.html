AAJ Index Atlas

The American Alpine Journal (AAJ) master index, parsed line by line into people, places, and page references, then mapped to mountain regions. An index is a record of what an editor and an indexer thought worth finding again, so every chart below says something about climbing and something about bookkeeping. Part of the fun is telling which is which.

Figure 1 · What the index counts

References per volume, split by what kind of entry they belong to

Each bar is one AAJ volume. A reference is one "year: page" citation under one index entry, so a single report page about a climb usually spawns several: the peak, each climber, the range. The split between people and places is where the indexer's hand shows.

Photo-caption references are excluded here (they were indexed in some eras and not others). Places = entries geolocated to a region; Topics = clubs, equipment, accidents, medicine and similar.

People references fall from about two per place reference before 1955 to about one in three after 2016, and to nearly zero in 2021. Did climbers stop mattering to the AAJ, or did the indexing rules change? What would you look at to decide?

Figure 2 · Climbs by continent

Where the AAJ's climbing references point, year by year

Place references only, grouped by continent. Switch to share to remove the effect of fatter or thinner volumes and indexing depth, which is the fairer comparison across eras.

North America falls from 72% of place references (1929–55) to 34% (2000–24); Asia rises from 7% to 42%. Europe drops from 10% to about 2% once the journal stopped reporting members' Alpine summers.

South America peaks at a third of all references in 1956–79, then gives ground to Asia. Is that Peru getting climbed out, or Asia opening up? Figure 4 is one way to test it.

Figure 4 · Geopolitics in the footnotes

Borders open and close, and the index notices

Six regions as a share of each volume's place references, with dated political events marked. An AAJ volume mostly reports the previous year's climbs, so expect the index to answer about a year late.

Event dates: Nepal's ban on Himalayan climbing (1965) and reopening of 38 peaks (March 1969), and China's 1980 opening to foreign expeditions, are from the Himalayan Journal. Noshaq's reopening is from National Geographic (2011). Others are standard historical dates. Everest and Kangchenjunga are counted under Nepal, so Nepal's pre-1950 values are the British Everest attempts made from Tibet and climbs approached from Sikkim.

Afghanistan has a twenty-year climbing era (about 1960–80) that ends almost to the year of the Soviet invasion, then a short revival around 2009–14 that fades years before the 2021 Taliban takeover. Peru has no political marker at all, yet it swings the hardest. What else besides access could drive a region's share?

Figure 5 · The frontier

New place names entering the index each year

For every place entry, the first volume that cites it. A steady supply of first mentions means the journal is still reporting new ground rather than revisiting the classics. Region-level entries such as "Nepal" or "Peru" are left out.

The 1985 spike is one article listing dozens of Nepal peaks by elevation (Peak 6055, Peak 6059 and so on, all on pages 116–134). The 1973 spike is mostly Peruvian cordilleras.

Figure 6 · Famous mountains

References per volume for 22 well-known peaks

Each row is one index entry; stronger cells mean more references that year. Some mountains are steady background presences, others arrive with a generation.

Denali and Everest are almost always present, while Cerro Torre and FitzRoy become regulars only from about 1970, and Trango Tower and Thor (Baffin) from the late 1970s and 1980s. Is that the difficulty frontier moving, or travel cost falling?

Figure 7 · The most-indexed people

Thirty careers, by the volumes that cite them

Each dot is one volume; dot area is the number of references that year. Ranked by total references.

Several of the most-indexed names are better known as chroniclers than as climbers: Elizabeth Hawley in Kathmandu, Kamal Guha and Harish Kapadia for India, Evelio Echevarría for the Andes, Józef Nyka for Poland, Lindsay Griffin. What does that say about whose eyes the AAJ saw the world through, and when?

Figure 8 · How thick was the book?

The highest page number cited in each volume

A rough proxy for volume length: the 99.5th-percentile page cited, which ignores stray typos. Before 1981 the value swings by a factor of two from one year to the next, which looks more like a pagination or indexing quirk than real changes in length.

From 1981 the series is steady at about 340–400 pages, climbs to about 480 for 1999–2008, then steps back to about 385 in 2009 and stays there. A redesign, a bigger page, or a real cut? Putting the 2008 and 2009 volumes side by side would settle it.

Method and caveats

How the numbers were made

  • Parsing. The PDF is a Google Sheets export with two columns. Text was read with its position on the page, so each entry name stays with its references even when either column wraps. Bold "YYYY:" markers start a year group; the page numbers after them are references. Ranges like "312-4" were expanded to 312–314. Check: 99.9% of the "YYYY:" markers in the raw text landed in a parsed entry.
  • Classification. "Surname, Given" with no parentheses counts as a person. Anything with a parenthetical location, such as "(Cordillera Blanca, Peru)", is a place and is matched to a region by about 600 keyword patterns, reading the last segment first. A random sample of 50 entries came out about 98% right. Border peaks are assigned by rule: Everest, Cho Oyu and Makalu to Nepal; K2, Broad Peak and the Gasherbrums to Pakistan.
  • What a reference is not. It is not a climb or a report. One report produces several references, and indexing depth changes by era. Shares within a year are more trustworthy than raw counts across years.
  • Known artifacts. People were barely indexed in the 2021 volume. Photo captions were hardly indexed from 1976 to 1986 or in 1997. Elevation-named peaks ("Peak 6055") appear only in some eras. Elevation-style numbers over 1,000 were kept out of page counts (566 of them).
